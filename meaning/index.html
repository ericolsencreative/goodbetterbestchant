What "Good, Better, Best" Actually Means

It sounds like a pep rhyme. It's closer to a rule: the day you win is the day you're most likely to stop improving, so that's the day you say it.

Last updated November 7, 2026

Line by line

Good, better, best.
Three levels, in order. Good is where you are. It's not an insult; it's a starting point.
Never let it rest.
The instruction. Don't settle at any of the three. "Rest" here means complacency, not sleep.
'Til your good gets better,
The first step up. Whatever you did well, do it better.
And your better gets best.
The second step. And the rhyme doesn't say what happens after best, because the loop just starts again.

Why only after wins

The Bears don't chant it after losses. That's the part most people miss. After a loss nobody needs reminding to improve; after a win, everybody does. Johnson built the chant into the one moment a team is most tempted to relax, so the celebration and the warning arrive together (ESPN). Players describe it the same way: good is not good enough, and the message hits hardest in the weeks after you've been told you're good (ABC7 / ESPN).

Why it works as a chant

  • It rhymes and it's short. Four lines, fifteen seconds, no one forgets it.
  • It's call and response. Saying it back out loud is a small act of agreement. You can't mumble it.
  • It's scarce. You only get to do it when you've earned it, so it stays a reward.
  • It praises improvement, not winning. Which is why it travels so easily out of football and into classrooms and offices.

Outside football

The rhyme predates the Bears by more than a century and was a classroom staple for most of the 20th century (who said it). It's a growth-mindset lesson in sixteen words: effort is the variable, and the job is never finished. Teachers use it as a writing prompt ("describe a time your good got better"). Manufacturing and quality consultants use it as a one-line description of continuous improvement. Parents use it the way a Mississippi mother did, sending a first-grader out the door with it (Mt. Olive Baptist).

One Chicago writer put the balance well: it doesn't shame where you are. Good is allowed. Better is encouraged. Best is pursued. Rest is what's off the table (Jeremy Ferrero).

The honest caveat

"Never let it rest" is a line about complacency, not a license for burnout. The Bears say it, then go home. If you're using it with kids, say that part out loud: the chant is for the five seconds after a win, not for every waking hour.

Using it in a speech, a classroom or a meeting

  1. Say the four lines once, plainly, before you explain anything.
  2. Name the "good": the specific thing that went well. The chant is empty without it.
  3. Name one "better": one concrete thing to improve next time. One, not five.
  4. Close with the lines again, and if you have a group, make them say it back.

FAQ

What does "good, better, best, never let it rest" mean?

Don't settle at any level. Whatever you did well, do it better; whatever you did better, make it your best. Good is the starting point, not the goal.

Why do the Bears only say it after wins?

Because the temptation to coast comes after winning. The chant attaches a reminder to improve to the moment of celebration.

Is good, better, best a growth mindset quote?

Yes, in effect. It frames ability as something you improve through effort, which is the core of the growth-mindset idea, and it predates that term by about a century.

Is "never let it rest" about working nonstop?

No. It's about not becoming complacent. The Bears chant it for fifteen seconds and go home.
