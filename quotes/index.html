The "Good, Better, Best" Quote: Text, Cards and the Right Credit

Both wordings, ready to copy. Cards you can download. And the credit line that is actually true.

The quote

There are two standard wordings. The traditional one is what most quote pages carry; the Bears' version is what you'll hear in the clips.

Traditional

Good, better, best. Never let it rest. 'Til your good is better and your better is best.

The Bears' version

Good, better, best. Never let it rest. 'Til your good gets better, and your better gets best.

Tim Duncan's version

Good, better, best. Never let it rest. Until your good is better and your better is your best.

Every variant, and which one to use where, is on the words page.

Who to credit

Not St. Jerome, not Tim Duncan, not Thomas Hardy. The rhyme is a traditional English saying in print since at least 1897, author unknown. Duncan quoted it for his whole career and credited his mother. The full evidence is on who really said it. A credit line you can use:

— Traditional English rhyme, in print since 1897 (often misattributed to St. Jerome)

Using the quote

  • In a speech or toast: say it once, name the "good" you're celebrating, name one "better," say it again. The frame is on what it means.
  • In a classroom: the traditional wording, credited "Traditional." The misattribution is itself a good lesson.
  • On a wall or a shirt: free to print; it's a public-domain proverb. Team names, logos and league marks are not, so leave those off.
  • As a tattoo: people do, and the attribution question matters more when it's permanent. See the tattoo page.

FAQ

What is the good, better, best quote?

"Good, better, best. Never let it rest. 'Til your good is better and your better is best." The Bears' version says "gets" instead of "is."

Who said "good, better, best, never let it rest"?

No one famous. It's a traditional English rhyme in print since 1897. It's widely but wrongly credited to St. Jerome; Tim Duncan quoted it and credited his mother.

Is the good, better, best quote copyrighted?

No. It's a traditional proverb more than a century old. You can print, share and quote it freely.
